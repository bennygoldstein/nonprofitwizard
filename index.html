Who we help

Thousands of nonprofits, every kind you can imagine

In more than 20 years our team has formed synagogues and temples, churches, dog shelters, soup kitchens, food pantries, schools, youth programs, and arts groups. Big or small, faith-based or secular, we specialize in doing it all. Each kind has its own rules, and the Wizard knows them.

Synagogues & temples

The IRS treats houses of worship as exempt even without applying. Many still want the official determination letter (the IRS's approval letter) so members and donors can give with confidence. The IRS requires the full Form 1023 from them, so we use our Long Form program and handle the extra schedule for churches, synagogues, and temples.

Churches & ministries

From a new congregation to a mission outreach, we prepare the Articles, bylaws, and full Form 1023 the IRS asks of churches. A determination letter gives donors, lenders, and grantmakers proof that your church is recognized and that gifts are deductible.

Animal shelters & rescues

Dog rescues and shelters run on donations, adoption fees, and volunteers. We prepare and file your 501(c)(3) application so that, once approved, donors can deduct their gifts and foundations that fund animal welfare can consider you. Many new rescues qualify for the Short Form.

Soup kitchens

Feeding people means partnering with food banks, restaurants, and grocers, and many of them require a recognized 501(c)(3) before they donate. We get you incorporated, your EIN in hand, and your application filed so meals can start moving.

Schools & educational programs

Private schools and preschools with a regular faculty, curriculum, and enrolled students must file the full Form 1023 (with its school schedule), and some states add steps. New York, for example, requires the Education Department's consent before it will incorporate a school. Many tutoring and after-school programs aren't schools in the IRS's eyes and can use the Short Form. We know the difference and build the right steps into your timeline.

Youth programs & sports leagues

Little leagues, after-school clubs, and mentoring programs collect registration fees and sponsorships. A 501(c)(3) lets families and local businesses support you with tax-deductible gifts and keeps the money in the program. Many leagues qualify for the Short Form.

Food pantries

Regional food banks, grant programs, and grocery partners usually ask for your IRS determination letter and EIN before they work with you. We prepare everything to be complete and accurate before it's filed, so a small pantry can be up and running on the Short Form.

Arts & culture

Community theaters, choirs, museums, and music programs rely on ticket sales plus grants from arts councils and foundations, which almost always require 501(c)(3) status. We write the mission and activity descriptions the IRS wants to see from cultural groups.

Why us

Why doing it alone is so hard (and why the Wizard exists)

Starting a nonprofit means satisfying two governments at once. Your state has its own rules for incorporation, board size, and required language. The IRS has its own forms, fees, and a long list of questions about your mission, money, and governance. Miss one detail and the IRS writes back for more information, which can add months. Leave enough unanswered and the IRS can close the file, keep the user fee, and make you start over. Most founders do this once in their lives. Our team has done it thousands of times.

Two forms, one wrong choice

Form 1023-EZ is short and low-cost, but only if you pass a 30-question IRS eligibility worksheet. Churches, schools, and groups expecting more than $50,000 a year must use the full Form 1023. Pick the wrong one and you start over.

Every state plays by its own rules

Minimum directors, filing fees, expedite options, and even newspaper notices differ from state to state. New York, for example, needs a state agency's written consent before it will incorporate certain schools and health organizations.

The exact words the IRS looks for

Your Articles must include a specific charitable purpose clause and a dissolution clause sending leftover assets to another 501(c)(3) or to government for a public purpose. Leave them out (unless your state's statute supplies them) and the IRS will hold your application until they're fixed and refiled with the state.

Your board matters more than you think

Most states require at least three directors, and the IRS looks hard at boards run by one person or one family. Unrelated directors, a conflict-of-interest policy, and real meetings are the practical standard.

The most common reasons for denial

Inaccurate information, a mission that isn't clearly charitable, and missing financial details top the list. All three are avoidable with careful preparation.

Approval is the start, not the finish

Nearly every nonprofit (churches are the exception) must file a Form 990-series return each year, even while the application is pending. Miss three years in a row and the IRS automatically revokes your status, with no appeal.

Already have an accountant? Good. We work with them.

Accountants are great at books and taxes, but not every accountant specializes in forming nonprofits or in the 501(c)(3) application. We handle the formation and the IRS filing, then hand your accountant a clean package: Articles, bylaws, EIN, determination letter, and a plain-English note on which Form 990 you'll owe each year. We're glad to answer their questions along the way. If your accountant would rather hand this piece off, that's exactly what we're here for.

The payoff

What 501(c)(3) status unlocks

Recognition from the IRS is more than a letter. It changes how much of every dollar reaches your mission, and who is willing to give in the first place. This is the part the wand was for.

Federal income tax exemption

Your nonprofit pays no federal income tax on money raised for its mission, so more of every dollar goes to the work instead of to taxes.

Tax-deductible donations

Donors can generally deduct their gifts (subject to their own tax situation), which makes asking easier and gifts bigger. Apply within 27 months of forming and, once approved, your exemption reaches back to your formation date, so gifts received while you waited count too.

Access to grants

Most private foundations and many government programs fund only organizations with an IRS determination letter. Without one, the door is closed before you knock.

State sales-tax exemption

Many states let recognized nonprofits buy supplies without paying sales tax. It usually takes a separate state application, which we can point you to, and a few states don't offer it at all.

Reduced nonprofit postage

The Postal Service offers lower Nonprofit Marketing Mail rates to qualifying religious, educational, charitable, and similar groups. Your IRS letter is strong evidence, but USPS makes its own decision on its authorization form (PS Form 3624), so approval isn't automatic.

Credibility that opens doors

Banks, landlords, partners, and volunteers take a recognized 501(c)(3) seriously. Your EIN and determination letter show that your organization is real, accountable, and here to stay.

The Wizard's glossary

Plain-English definitions of the words you'll meet along the way.

501(c)(3)

The section of the federal tax code covering charitable, religious, educational, scientific, and literary organizations. A recognized 501(c)(3) pays no federal income tax, and its donors can generally deduct their gifts.

EIN (Employer Identification Number)

Your nonprofit's nine-digit federal tax ID from the IRS. You need one to open a bank account and to apply for tax exemption, even with no employees. It's free and issued online.

Articles of Incorporation

The document filed with your state that legally creates the corporation. For a 501(c)(3) it must include a charitable purpose clause and a dissolution clause.

Bylaws

Your board's internal rulebook: how many directors, officer roles, how meetings and votes work, and how to make changes. The IRS expects them with your application.

Board of directors

The group legally responsible for the nonprofit. Most states require at least three; the IRS looks closely at any board controlled by one person or one family.

Conflict-of-interest policy

Written rules for what happens when a director or officer could personally benefit from a decision. The IRS asks on Form 1023 whether you have one.

Form 1023

The full IRS application for 501(c)(3) recognition, filed online through Pay.gov with a $600 user fee. Required for larger organizations and for all churches, schools, and hospitals.

Form 1023-EZ

The streamlined online application for small nonprofits that pass the IRS eligibility worksheet, filed on Pay.gov with a $275 user fee.

Determination letter

The IRS's official letter recognizing your nonprofit as tax-exempt. It's mailed to you and posted on the IRS's public search tool. Donors and grantmakers ask for it.

Form 990 (990-N, 990-EZ, 990)

The annual information return nearly every exempt organization must file (churches are the main exception). Which version you file depends on your size. It's due the 15th day of the fifth month after your year ends, May 15 for calendar-year groups.

Gross receipts

All the money your nonprofit takes in during a year from every source, before subtracting any expenses. The IRS uses it to decide which forms you qualify for.

Dissolution clause

Language in your Articles stating that if the nonprofit ever closes, its remaining assets go to another 501(c)(3) or to a government for a public purpose, never to individuals.

Private inurement

The rule that no insider may pocket the nonprofit's earnings. Directors and officers can be paid only reasonable amounts, and most serve as volunteers.

Registered agent

The person or company at a physical street address in your state who receives legal notices and official mail for the corporation. Most states require one at formation.

Pay.gov

The federal government's online payment site where Form 1023 and Form 1023-EZ are filed and the IRS user fee is paid. The IRS does not accept paper applications.

Which program do I need: the Short Form or the Long Form?
Short Form ($2,000) files IRS Form 1023-EZ, the streamlined application. It fits if you expect $50,000 or less a year in each of your first three years, hold under $250,000 in assets, and aren't a church, synagogue, school, or hospital. Long Form ($2,600) files the full Form 1023. The IRS requires it if you already have more than $50,000 to put in, expect more than $50,000 in any of your first three years, hold over $250,000 in assets, or are a house of worship, school, or hospital. Growing past $50,000 later is fine, as long as your projections were honest when you filed; you simply file a different annual return. Not sure? Tell the Wizard your plans and we'll confirm on the call.
What's included in the program?
Everything needed to go from an idea to a filed 501(c)(3) application: state Articles of Incorporation with the charitable-purpose and dissolution language the IRS requires; bylaws; a conflict-of-interest policy; your EIN; and Form 1023 or 1023-EZ, prepared by us and submitted through Pay.gov once your authorized officer has reviewed and signed it. You or a board member can usually serve as your own registered agent at no cost; we explain that on the call. If the IRS writes back for more information, we help you respond. Not included: government filing fees, annual Form 990 returns, and state charitable-solicitation registration. We tell you exactly what those cost before you pay anything.
What government fees will I pay on top of your fee?
In most states, two. First, the IRS user fee, paid on Pay.gov when we submit: $275 for Form 1023-EZ (Short Form) or $600 for Form 1023 (Long Form). It's non-refundable, and the IRS can change it each January. Second, your state's fee to file Articles of Incorporation, usually $25 to $170 (for example, $75 in New York, $70 in Florida, $30 in California). A few states add costs: Pennsylvania and Georgia require a newspaper notice, Maryland adds a $50 surcharge for 501(c)(3) applicants, and most states sell optional expedited filing. State charity registration and state tax exemption, if you pursue them later, have their own fees. We tell you the exact figures for your state before anything is filed.

How long does the whole thing take?
Once we have your information, we prepare and file everything within 2 weeks (10 business days); Rush does it in 5 business days for $99 more. Within about a week of filing with the state you typically have your EIN and state filing number, which is what you need to open a bank account. Your IRS application goes in as soon as the state confirms your incorporation. Approval is up to the government. Most states confirm incorporation in days to a few weeks. The IRS says it decides most 1023-EZ applications within about three to four weeks and most full Form 1023 applications within about six to seven months. These are the government's averages, not promises. So plan on a few weeks for the Short Form and six months or more for the Long Form, plus your state's incorporation time.
How many directors do I need?
Your state sets the minimum. Most states, including New York, New Jersey, Florida, Texas, Illinois, Ohio, and Connecticut, require at least three directors; a few, such as California, Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Delaware, allow just one. Whatever your state says, the IRS looks hard at boards controlled by one person or one family, so we ask every client for at least three directors who aren't related to each other. You can always add more later through your bylaws.
Can directors be paid?
Federal law allows it, but pay must be reasonable for the work actually done and reported on your Form 990. The safe way to prove it's reasonable: have board members who don't benefit approve it in advance, using comparable-pay data, and record it in the minutes. Overpaying an insider can trigger IRS penalty taxes on that person and put your exemption at risk. Some states also limit how many paid people may sit on a board. Most nonprofit directors serve as volunteers, and for a new organization that's what we recommend.
Do I really need to file a Form 990 every year?
Yes, every year, starting with the year you form, even while your application is pending. Groups with gross receipts normally $50,000 or less file the short electronic Form 990-N e-Postcard. Larger ones file Form 990-EZ or the full Form 990. All are due by the 15th day of the fifth month after your fiscal year ends (May 15 for calendar-year groups). Miss three years in a row and the IRS automatically revokes your exemption with no appeal; you'd have to reapply and pay the user fee again. Churches and synagogues are the exception: they aren't required to file.
What is an EIN?
An Employer Identification Number is the nine-digit federal tax ID the IRS assigns to your organization; think of it as a Social Security number for the nonprofit. You need one even if you never hire anyone: banks require it to open an account, and the IRS won't accept Form 1023 or 1023-EZ without it. The IRS issues it free online, usually the same day we apply, once your state has accepted your Articles. Obtaining it is part of every Nonprofit Wizard program.
Can the IRS reject my application?
It can, but outright denial is rare. Far more often the IRS sends a letter asking for more information, and we help you answer it. Denials usually come from a mission that doesn't fit 501(c)(3)'s charitable, religious, educational, or scientific purposes, inaccurate answers, an unclear mission statement, or missing financial details. Those are exactly the things we screen for before filing. No one can guarantee IRS approval; the IRS alone decides. When it says yes, it mails your determination letter and posts it on the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search.
What if the IRS says no, or I change my mind?
If we review your idea and think it won't qualify, we tell you up front, before you pay. If the IRS writes back with questions, we help you answer them; if it denies the application, we help you fix and refile it. We go over our refund and cancellation terms with you on the call, before any payment, so there are no surprises.
Do churches and synagogues need to apply?
Not strictly. Churches, synagogues, temples, and mosques that meet the 501(c)(3) requirements are automatically treated as tax-exempt and don't have to apply or file annual 990 returns. Many still apply anyway, because an IRS determination letter is what gives members, donors, and grantmakers proof that gifts are tax-deductible. One catch: houses of worship can't use the streamlined 1023-EZ, so they go through our Long Form program with the full Form 1023, something our team has done for many congregations.

We take care of the incorporation, bylaws, policies, and IRS application. Your accountant supplies or reviews the budgets and projections the form asks for. After approval, we hand everything back to them for bookkeeping and annual Form 990 filings, along with a plain-English note on which return you'll owe each year.
