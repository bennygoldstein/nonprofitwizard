Before You Start a Nonprofit: Seven Decisions to Make

First weekly article · October 21, 2026 · 7 min read · Nonprofit Wizard

A practical starting point for turning a charitable idea into a clear purpose, a workable first program, and a responsible organization.

A neighbor needs a meal. A child needs a safe place after school. An animal needs a home. Many nonprofits begin with a need someone notices and cannot ignore. The first useful move is to make that idea specific enough that other people can help you carry it out.

Before you choose a form, spend an hour with the seven decisions below. You can write rough answers. The point is to find the questions that need a conversation, research, or a change of plan.

1. Whom will you help, and how?

Describe a real group of people or a clear community need. Then name the activity you will deliver. “Help children succeed” is a wish. “Offer free Saturday reading sessions for elementary pupils in our neighborhood” gives your future board something it can plan and measure.

The IRS recognizes specific exempt purposes, including charitable, religious, and educational purposes. An appealing idea and a qualifying charitable purpose are not always the same thing. Read the IRS's purpose categories and check how your activities fit.

Try this: “We help [people or community] by [specific activity] in [place or setting].” Treat this as a working sentence, not final legal language for your articles.

2. Do you need a new organization?

Find out who is already working on the problem. Talk to a local pantry, school, rescue, congregation, or community group. A partnership may let you test your idea sooner, learn from an experienced team, and spend more time helping people.

Creating a separate organization brings its own records, board decisions, finances, and reporting. It may be the right choice. Make it a deliberate choice rather than the automatic first step.

Write down: one organization to contact, one possible shared activity, and what a separate nonprofit would add.

3. Who will share responsibility?

A board is more than a list of names. Think about who will review the budget, ask difficult questions, keep meetings moving, and help when a volunteer cancels. Look for people who bring different useful skills and can put the organization's interests first.

State rules determine minimum board requirements. Independent oversight and a practical conflict-of-interest process are sound governance practices; there is no single board-size rule that applies to every U.S. charity. Our board and bylaws guide helps you plan the conversation.

Ask each prospective director: What can you realistically contribute, how often can you meet, and what financial or family relationships should the board understand?

4. What will the first program actually look like?

Choose one manageable activity. For a reading program, decide where sessions will happen, how many children can attend safely, who will supervise, and what materials are needed. For a food project, ask about storage, transport, food safety, and reliable supply.

Make a simple first-program sheet: date, location, participants, responsible people, equipment, permissions to check, and the result you hope to see. Local rules, insurance, safeguarding, and professional requirements can matter independently of tax status.

A small, well-prepared activity can teach you more than a long list of programs you cannot yet staff.

5. What does a realistic starting budget say?

List expected income and expenses separately. Include ordinary costs such as space, supplies, insurance, transport, bookkeeping, and official filing fees. Identify which money is already available, which is pledged, and which is still a hope.

Then ask a harder question: what happens if half the expected donations do not arrive? You might begin with fewer sessions, borrow equipment, share space, or wait until a partner is ready. A modest plan you can sustain is useful.

Keep your assumptions. Your future board should be able to see why the numbers were chosen and what would make you revise them.

6. Which decisions belong to the state, and which to the IRS?

State incorporation creates a corporation under state law. Federal recognition concerns tax-exempt status under federal law. They are distinct. Charitable fundraising registration and state tax exemptions can involve additional steps.

Read the startup roadmap before deciding which application applies. Organizing documents need appropriate purpose and asset-dedication provisions. Avoid copying another group's paperwork without checking your state and your proposed activities.

You do not have to memorize the process. You do need a list of the official offices and instructions relevant to your organization.

7. Who will keep the organization on track?

Give every continuing task a person and a place on the calendar: financial records, board minutes, state renewals, and any federal returns or notices. Plan where your documents will live and who will have access when a board member leaves.

Ask how the board will know the program is helping. For the reading example, you could track sessions delivered, attendance, parent feedback, and what your volunteer team learns. Keep the measures proportionate to the program.

Your next useful hour

  1. Write your one-sentence purpose.
  2. List three people to speak with: a possible partner, a possible director, and someone who understands the community need.
  3. Draft the first-program sheet and a simple budget.
  4. Use the free startup checklist to record the questions you still have.

You can bring those questions to our free consultation. A clear question is a good place to begin.
