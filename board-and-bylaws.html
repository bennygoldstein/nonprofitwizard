Build a Board That Can Do the Work

Free learning guide Â· October 21, 2026 Â· 4 min read Â· Nonprofit Wizard

Practical questions for choosing directors, planning meetings, writing bylaws, and handling conflicts of interest.

Think of the board as the group responsible for keeping the organization faithful to its purpose. Directors need enough information, time, and independence to make decisions rather than simply approve what a founder proposes.

Choose for commitment and useful skills

Consider people who understand the community, finances, practical program delivery, or organizational management. Ask about the time they can give. Check your state's rules for minimum directors and any restrictions. Independent perspectives can help the board recognize conflicts and make decisions in the charity's interests.

Have an honest first conversation

  • What will the organization do in its first year, and what will it leave for later?
  • Who will review income, expenses, and bank activity?
  • How often can directors meet and what information will they receive?
  • Does anyone have a family, employment, or business relationship relevant to a decision?
  • Who will keep minutes and safeguard organizational records?

Use bylaws as a practical rulebook

Bylaws commonly address director terms, officers, meetings, notice, voting, quorum, vacancies, and amendments. They should fit your state law and actual way of working. Do not copy an unfamiliar organization's provisions just because they look formal. Ask whether your board could follow each provision in practice.

Write a conflict-of-interest process

A conflict can arise when a decision could benefit a director, a relative, or a related business. A practical policy explains disclosure, who evaluates the situation, how interested people step back from the decision, and what is recorded. Tailor the policy to the organization. Its value comes from using it consistently.

Keep a clear record

Minutes should show the material decisions and who made them. For significant financial commitments, retain the budget, information considered, and reasons for the decision. Give incoming directors the organizing documents, bylaws, current budget, key policies, and calendar.

Board meeting exercise: imagine the organization needs to rent a room owned by a director's relative. Discuss how you would disclose the relationship, compare options, decide who can vote, and record the outcome.

The IRS encourages thoughtful governance practices; that does not mean every suggested policy or board structure is a universal federal legal requirement. State law and your circumstances still matter.
